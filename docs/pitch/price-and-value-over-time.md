## The one-line story (headline for the slide)

**Domino is paid as a share of the value it creates — a share that starts
services-heavy while the solution is built and proven, and shifts to a
license as the automation becomes a durable capability the Navy owns and
runs itself.** The price goes up in absolute terms as scope and value
scale; the *fee as a percentage of value delivered* goes down every year,
because a mature platform scales value faster than it scales cost.

---

## The problem this prices against

- Today's redesign cycle — from chronic-failure identification to a
  contractually approved business case — takes **6–18 months** and
  routinely costs **$250K–$750K+ in direct labor** and **10–30 people**
  per major component, per Class I ECP.
- A naval enterprise manages **thousands of equipment families and
  subsystems across dozens of hulls** — this cost and cycle time repeats
  every time, with no centralized, end-to-end view of the process.
- **FATHOM Redesign Console** collapses that cycle to **~6 days** by
  giving operators one system that tracks a failure from identification
  through causal attribution to a costed, evidence-backed business case.

## The 5-year engagement model: services-heavy → license-heavy

The engagement is structured on Domino's solutions model: a **declining
forward-deployed engineering (FDE) services fee** that builds and proves
the capability, transitioning to a **rising platform license** as that
capability becomes a repeatable, owned, self-sustaining part of how the
Navy runs its redesign process — the same "build it once, then it's
yours to run" arc already reflected in this program's own architecture
(a proven vertical-slice build pattern, meant to be repeated across the
remaining domain and platform services without repeating the Year-1
build cost each time).

**5-year cumulative:** ~$26M in Domino fees against **~$377M in
cumulative value delivered — a blended ~14x return**, rising to **~20x
annually by Year 5** as the fee-to-value ratio shrinks from ~20% in Year 1
(justified by the heavy, one-time build investment) to ~5% by Year 5 (a
mature, largely self-running platform).

## Why the mix shifts this way (the argument behind the numbers)

- **Year 1 is expensive per unit of value on purpose.** Building the
  first working vertical slice — the shared data model, the security and
  auth patterns, the deployment pipeline — is the highest-cost, most
  bespoke work in the entire engagement. It is also the work that makes
  every subsequent service cheaper to build, which is exactly why its
  cost shows up as a services fee, not a license fee: the Navy is paying
  for engineering effort that does not repeat.
- **Every year after, less new engineering buys more new value**, because
  the platform pattern proven in Year 1 is reused, not rebuilt, for each
  additional equipment family, ship class, and program. That's the
  mechanical reason the FDE share can decline without value delivery
  slowing down.
- **The license share rises because what the Navy is paying for changes
  in kind, not just in degree** — from "engineers building us a system"
  to "a running capability we now own and operate, that Domino continues
  to host, sustain, and improve." That transition is the whole point of
  structuring this as a share of value rather than a flat services rate:
  Domino's incentive stays aligned with value delivered instead of hours
  billed, in both directions of the curve.
- **Total value delivered keeps growing even as Domino's percentage
  shrinks**, because scope keeps expanding — more equipment families,
  more ship classes, then the add-on products (an automated design
  platform, automated SOW/WBS/BOM/scheduling) layer additional value on
  top of the same durable platform investment, at close to zero
  incremental Year-1-style build cost.

## Speaker notes / one-slide takeaway

> "In Year 1, most of what you're paying for is us building and proving
> this. By Year 5, most of what you're paying for is a license to run a
> capability that's already yours — one that's grown from a single ship
> class to the fleet, and from redesign case-building to a broader
> automation platform. The price goes up because the value goes up faster
> — and the share we take of that value goes *down* every year."
